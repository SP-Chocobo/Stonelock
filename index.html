How Stonelock Is Played

You are seizing assets. At the showdown you field any three cards from your final table layout. Matching cards earn flat bonuses; Interaction Stones warp the board. Card values shift with the venue — see The Venues below.

The Hand

  1. The Deal — five cards each from a fresh-shuffled pool.
  2. The Foundation — each player telegraphs a stone, then both commit 2 cards face-up.
  3. The Veil — second stone telegraphed (reverse order); both commit 1 card face-down.
  4. The Final Commitment — third stone telegraphed; 1 final face-down card. The leftover hand card is discarded dead.
  5. The Thinning — each player abandons one telegraphed stone, keeping two active.
  6. Stone Resolutions — in deal order, each player places their first stone, then their second.
  7. The Showdown — all cards flip; best 3 of your 4 are scored.

Scoring

Add the regional values of your three fielded cards. Pair (two identical): +2. Triad (three identical): +6. A phantom duplicate from a Red Stone may stand as one of your three for the Pair/Triad bonus, but it adds no point value of its own — a weak phantom may not even make your scoring set. Ties break by structure — Triad beats Pair beats Singles; identical structure is a Push and the marker holds.

The Interaction Stones

  • Red — Duplication. A phantom duplicate rides one of your cards, standing in as the second or third copy for a Pair or Triad bonus. The phantom scores no points itself — insurance on your Triad, not a free one.
  • White — Lock. The card cannot be altered, stolen, or neutralized for the rest of the hand.
  • Blue — Exchange. Forcibly swap one of your cards for an unprotected opponent card. Face-up stays face-up; face-down stays face-down, but you may secretly inspect a hidden card that enters your layout.
  • Black — Disruption. Played on your turn like any other stone: it undoes the last stone effect upon a card — including unwinding a trade, with every riding stone traveling home. Cannot itself be undone, and cannot touch a locked card.

Formats & Scoring

No betting at these tables — all honor play. The Dealer Token rotates every hand.

  • Solo 1v1 / Paired Teams: the Ledger Stone shifts by the net difference between the two sides each showdown. Push it the full distance to take the match. In teams, your partner sits opposite; team totals decide the showdown, but Pairs and Triads never pool across layouts. Partners may Lock each other's cards and never trade against each other.
  • Free-for-All (4 seats): a purse race — each showdown, every seat banks its margin over the lowest hand (hands of 7/8/10/12 bank 0/1/3/5). First to the target takes the match; if leaders cross the line together, the race runs on until one stands alone.
  • Real players (hotseat): any table can seat two, three, or four real players sharing one device — pick how many under The players, and bots fill the rest. A pass screen hides the table between turns so veiled cards stay private.
  • The company: keep the usual seats, draw lots from whoever is in the room, or choose your table seat by seat. Watch the epithets — not every regular plays equally well.
  • Open Table targeting (advanced): any stone may target any layout, allies included — red an ally's pair, lock a rival's dead card in place, or trade between any two seats.
  • The deals: Small Game — 5 cards, a 2-1-1 footprint, best 3 of 4. House Deep Draft — 9 cards, a 2-2-1 footprint, best 3 of 5, leftovers discarded dead.

Raid Boss — The Magistrate

A co-op fight, reached from the title's Raid Boss button. You and an ally (a bot, or a second player in co-op hotseat) take the standard game — five cards, three stones each. The Magistrate is a single boss who fields a larger board (seven cards, all face-up), telegraphs its stones from a deep three-of-each pouch, and scores its two best non-overlapping three-card hands. Every action goes round the table — you commit and telegraph in turn, the boss answering — so its board and its stone picks reveal as you play. In The Reckoning, stones are spent from the telegraphed pool; the Magistrate answers every move and keeps the last word. Your two hand scores combine into a party total; the marker moves by (party − Magistrate) each showdown, and the Magistrate holds any tie. Drive it the full distance to break the boss.

Difficulty sets how many stones the boss spends: Easy (5), Standard (6 — a true coin-flip against good play), Hardcore (7 — it opens, answers, and closes). Coordinate: gang your six stones to shatter one of its two engines, and lock your own scorers before it strips them.

Two bosses sit the high seat. The Magistrate plays the fight as described above. The Warden is the same, but every stone spent — yours and its — is exhausted for a hand before it returns; it keeps one of every stone within reach and spends ruthlessly. Ration your disruption, or be ground down.

A locked card shields more than itself: if either card of a trade is later locked, a Black Stone can no longer unwind that trade.

The Venues

  • The Roadside Tavern — the Practical Common: Bread, Coin, Road high (3); Sword, Ferry mid (2); Quill, Crest, Chain low (1). No house rules.
  • The Sovereign Court — statecraft values: Chain, Crest, Quill high; Ferry, Road mid; Bread, Coin, Sword low. Formal play, no deviations.
  • The River Docks — the Fluvial Exchange: Road, Ferry, Coin high; Bread, Sword mid; Chain, Quill, Crest low. Riverlock: your final three must field a Road or Ferry, or the hand is docked 2 points after bonuses.
  • The Gambling Hall — common values, under the Cursed Register: each hand one card type is drawn cursed — it scores nothing and builds no Pairs or Triads, though stones may still target it.
  • The Slum Tables — common values, under Slumlock: a stone placed during resolution is exhausted for the next two hands before it returns to your pouch. Spend the pouch with a plan.
  • The Academy Gauntlet — common values, but a different game: no telegraphing and no thinning. Every player holds one of each stone and must place all four, one per round, in serpentine turn order (deal order, then reversed, and so on). The cards are a fixed canvas — it's a drill in pure interaction.
